Synthetic Data Needs a Chain of Custody

Synthetic data is the cheapest way to cover a gap in a dataset and the easiest way to fool yourself about a model. My working position is that synthetic data is useful when it stays tied to approved seed data and is never allowed to certify itself, and that the quality of the evaluation matters at least as much as the quality of the data.

A factory with a leash

In Mesh Brain, a synthetic data generator (NVIDIA NeMo DataDesigner) is permitted as a controlled factory for supervised rows, preference pairs, eval prompts, red-team prompts, benchmark task rows, and operator scenario packs. Its job is coverage and reproducibility, not authority.

The contract is as much about what is forbidden as what is allowed. Synthetic output cannot support a production-authority claim, a breakthrough claim, a "synthetic-only promotion," a live replication claim without a real endpoint result, operator-validation admission without real operator results, or a real-system pilot admission without observed real-system cases. In plain terms: synthetic cases may prepare an experiment, and only real observations may close one.

The hybrid part

"Hybrid" here means every synthetic row is paired with something real that can contradict it. The canonical dataset artifact carries a config, a seed manifest, generated rows, and a validated set. The provider boundary prefers a local or loopback OpenAI-compatible endpoint. External providers require an explicit approval marker, telemetry is disabled, and no private runtime data leaves without approval.

Data also carries lineage. Post-training manifests record lineage, tenant isolation, audit-only exclusion, model cards, and rollback metadata. Audit-only rows cannot silently become trainable rows. This is the part that matters most when synthetic and real rows are mixed, because contamination between a test set and a training set is nearly impossible to see afterward.

Why evaluation has to come first

Three results from my own experiments taught me that a data pipeline is only as honest as the instrument that grades it.

  • Two graders, same outputs, a factor of three apart. On a safeguard test, a lexical refusal proxy matched 145 of 939 responses (15.4%, 95% interval 13.3 to 17.9) against a 90% target, while a separate assessor scored 53.6% effective safe on the harmful set and flagged 62% of benign contrast prompts as unsafe. Neither instrument gave a calibrated verdict.
  • A null model that passes the test. In one replacement experiment, random and zero blocks retained 100% of task accuracy. The target was not behaviorally indispensable, so passing said nothing. Every evaluation set should be run against a null baseline before it is trusted.
  • An objective that was gamed by being easy. A first governed-improvement objective scored 0.750000, which I recorded as a metric-concentration finding rather than a capability gain. A balanced version requires persistence, calibration, and error correction each to improve by at least 0.04, plus an improved harmonic mean. It lifted the aggregate from 0.718000 to 0.745133 (3.7789%), and a below-floor candidate was rejected and reverted.

Synthetic data amplifies all three failure modes, because it is generated by the same family of systems that will be graded on it. That is the strongest argument I know for keeping real, custodied assessment data out of the generator's reach.

Evaluation beyond the usual benchmark

Conventional capability benchmarks answer "how good is it." The questions I care about are different, and I built gates for them:

  • Does it still answer correctly once its context is removed?
  • Does it prefer stale memory over a superseding fact?
  • Does any tenant's data leak into another tenant's answer?
  • Does it recommend an action the policy does not allow?
  • Do its citations drift from the evidence?
  • Does the same input replay to the same hash?

These are dependency-free instruments, with lexical, graph, compact-summary, and recurrent-state baselines to beat. The runtime-quality training lane uses a fixed answer contract (finding, evidence, policy boundary, recommended next step, whether approval is required, and citation references). The seed corpus is intentionally non-final. It exists to test source coverage and approval-boundary learning before anyone scales training.

What this does not establish

I have not shown that synthetic augmentation improves a downstream model. What exists is the contract, the gates, and the artifact layout that would let that claim be tested without contaminating the test set. That is a smaller thing than a result, and I would rather say so.
