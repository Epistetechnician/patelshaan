Who Vouches for the Data and the Compute?

A model is only as trustworthy as the data it learned from and the machine that ran it, and today both are vouched for by whoever operates them. I have been prototyping what it takes to make those claims independently checkable, so that a community can do work together without having to take any one party's word for it.

Buyer-controlled compute

HyperAI models a compute market where the buyer holds control: offers, buyer constraints, deals, fulfillment receipts, and mock settlement, all modeled locally and synthetically before any provider, payment, or fleet action exists. HyperIntel narrows this to inference. Offers are signed, reliability is expressed as typed artifacts with exact count-to-PPM arithmetic, and routing optimizes the buyer's expected successful cost rather than platform revenue. Provider price and platform fee are separate signed fields, and hidden spread is prohibited by the economics contract.

Outcome protocol: verifying that work was done

The harder question is how to settle on a claim about computation. The protocol freezes a claim, observes the result, runs it through pinned validators, allows disputes, finalizes, and prepares settlement. The interesting behavior is in the failure paths. Incomplete observation, replay divergence, and runner errors all resolve to unresolved with no settlement artifact. Settlement is idempotent, and the intent is persisted before submission so a crash recovers cleanly. In a dispute with a counterexample, a provisional false is refused and the claim later finalizes true with a single mock effect. Two gaps remain named in the record: live container timeout classification and composed stale-binding refusal.

Offline-checkable receipts

In HyperIntel, route, usage, settlement, and evidence identities are derived, so any tampering with them is rejected offline against a separately supplied trust registry. The point is the direction of trust. An independent party can check a receipt without calling the operator, which is the property a decentralized network needs from its participants. The accompanying custody contract is equally clear about what is not yet protected: the replay store is a local file that is not encrypted, replicated, or tenant-isolated, and the harness makes no confidentiality or no-retention claim.

Provenance for training data

The Mesh Brain data refinery turns mixed operational sources into supervised, preference, trajectory, eval, red-team, and provenance rows with source-aware envelopes. It is built around the questions a decentralized data market would face: can mixed sources become auditable training data without cross-tenant leakage, without audit-only rows becoming trainable, and without losing where each row came from? Post-training manifests carry lineage, tenant isolation, and rollback metadata, and release gates remain required before anything is promoted.

Tamper-evident run history

Orbital Mesh computes Merkle roots over canonical run events and returns a proof for each. Anyone holding a root can check that a given event was part of the history and that the history was not quietly edited. The same append-only structure underlies the evidence ledgers in the other repositories, where a corrected outcome supersedes rather than rewrites.

Incentives: the open part

Provenance and verification are the technical half. The economic half is who gets paid for contributing clean data, challenging bad data, or re-running a computation to check it. I have written about public-goods funding and capital flows in earlier essays, and the settlement layer in these prototypes is a zero-value mock on purpose. I have not run a real incentive experiment. That is the next research question and not a result.

What this does not establish

No live market, no real settlement, no decentralized network, and no measured incentive effect. Everything here is local and synthetic. What exists is the shape of the verification and provenance primitives such a system would need, with their limits written next to them.
