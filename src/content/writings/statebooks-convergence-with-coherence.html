Statebooks: Convergence with Coherence

Finance is converging in surface area before it has converged in meaning. A perpetual, an event contract, an option spread, and a tokenized claim can all point at the same world while carrying different observation rules, payoff shapes, collateral paths, default regimes, and finality conditions. Seeing them beside one another is useful. Treating them as interchangeable is how a legible interface becomes a dangerous one.

A Statebook starts with a smaller ambition than an exchange, clearing house, or universal oracle. It asks what each contract means in the states where it pays, then retains the differences that remain. The result is a coherence layer above federated books: a place to normalize material terms, compare state-dependent payoffs, expose residual risk, and report whether a proposed position is actually executable, capital-recognized, settlement-compatible, adequately assured, and recoverable.

Convergence begins with the terms

The useful insight behind market convergence is that more risks can be represented, traded, and composed. Compute capacity, power, model performance, policy, data access, and infrastructure constraints are becoming economically legible. But representation is only the first step. A position still has to answer which exact conditions cause each leg to pay, whether the comparison is exact or approximate, and whether an apparent offset survives execution, margin, settlement, and recovery failure.

This is why an event claim and a call spread cannot be called equivalent merely because they share a reference. Settlement source, comparator, observation window, payout, rounding, correction policy, dispute path, and finality domain all belong to the contract's material identity. A perpetual makes the point sharper: funding, marks, collateral, liquidation, and exit timing make it a continuing hedge profile, not a terminal claim with an omitted expiry. Statebook records those facts in a StateKey and reports a bounded residual instead of forcing a false identity.

Coherence is a vector, not a green light

A usable comparison must stay honest when one required condition is absent. Statebook therefore treats semantic, payoff, execution, capital, settlement, assurance, and recovery completeness as seven typed reports. Each carries its own status, evidence references, missing facts, and residuals. There is no aggregate complete = true capable of hiding a failed layer behind strong performance elsewhere.

A relationship may be semantically clear while its residual remains material. A residual may be bounded while market depth is insufficient. Execution can be feasible while no governing margin regime recognizes the legs as an offset. Even when models agree, current evidence, operational reconciliation, legal entitlement, or recovery paths may still be absent. Coherence is earned across the relevant conditions; it is not implied by a shared ticker, correlation, or polished quote screen.

Keep the market fast where risk can still be contained

The other half of coherence is temporal. Execution finality, economic settlement, and externalization finality are different clocks. A match can be irrevocable inside a market engine before balances are economically usable; a balance can update inside a recovery domain before value has crossed an external boundary that is hard to stop or reverse. Collapsing those clocks produces false confidence.

The design does not make every market slower. It keeps valid internal risk updates and genuinely all-or-none linked exchanges distinct from unilateral externalization. On the irreversible edge, hard evidence gates determine whether an action can proceed, exposure budgets determine how much, risk clocks determine when, and a queue determines what happens next. A favorable history or identity signal can reduce friction only inside that envelope; it cannot compensate for an unknown authorization, stale evidence, failed solvency check, or missing reconciliation path.

The point is not to make every market slower. It is to make the irreversible edge of a system proportionate to the evidence that has actually arrived.

Economic offset is not capital or legal recognition

Two positions can have nearly opposite payoffs while still demanding gross collateral. They may sit in different accounts, legal entities, customer-protection regimes, currencies, default funds, jurisdictions, or close-out netting sets. A Statebook can calculate an economic residual; it cannot award margin relief. The relevant clearing or margin authority must name the eligibility, model, stress scenarios, haircuts, liquidation rules, and conditions under which recognition is withdrawn. Technical confirmation likewise does not settle the separate question of legal finality.

A semantic core, not a universal authority

The proposed architecture keeps its layers legible: declared source terms and provenance; normalized contracts, StateKeys, and payoff functions; residual and completeness analysis; assurance and recovery policy; then separately governed execution, clearing, custody, pause, signing, and settlement systems. HSAI can supply narrow evidence and decision-envelope adapters. It does not turn an analysis result into market authority.

A bounded research direction

This is architecture research, not a live trading product or a claim that fragmented markets can be made complete by software alone. The current Statebook work is deliberately bounded to publication, source terms, synthetic fixtures, exact local analysis, and explicit non-claims. It does not execute trades, recognize margin, hold collateral, authorize a release, pause a venue, sign a transaction, or settle value.

That limitation is not a footnote. It is the core design premise. Coherence comes from retaining the distinctions that matter until the relevant authority, evidence, and finality conditions can support a stronger action. The first useful product is therefore read-only: normalize a bounded source set, report exact or approximate residuals, show all seven completeness dimensions, simulate policy, and export an audit bundle. Any authority surface needs its own owner, threat model, legal review, operational evidence, loss limits, and explicit authorization.
