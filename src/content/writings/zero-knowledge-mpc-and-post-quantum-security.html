Proofs, Privacy, and the Quantum Clock

Writing about the corner of your work where you have the least finished ground to stand on is an odd, revealing exercise. If AI infrastructure is ever going to serve people without asking them to trust whoever owns the servers, it needs real privacy, verifiable computation, and signatures built to outlast a quantum adversary. Of everything in my notebooks, this cryptographic basement is where the scaffolding is still rawest—which makes it the exact place where distinguishing built artifacts from open hopes matters most.

Scoring soundness before speed in zero knowledge

Scroll through most zero-knowledge leaderboards and you would think proving time is the only virtue that exists. Yet a blazing-fast prover that quietly accepts a forged witness is infinitely more dangerous than a sluggish prover that slams the door shut. Composed ZK Benchmark OS began from that simple inversion: instead of timing hand-picked circuits, start from program meaning—state machines, transitions, guards, loops, invariants, and expected accept-or-reject outcomes.

The harness generates workloads across nine families, injects 14 classes of adversarial trace mutations with known ground-truth verdicts, replays them locally, and keeps performance tables strictly separate from soundness evidence. Even its own limitations are wired into the output: an oracle-completeness audit and distinguishability matrix show exactly which generated constructs the shipped oracle can and cannot judge. Everything here sits candidly at Level 1—local, deterministic infrastructure with no external zkVM backend scores yet.

Eighty phases of humbling formal work

Formal methods have a wonderful way of puncturing ego. What actually exists on the formal side of that repository is compact and honest: one real local Z3 unsat proof for a narrow digest-binding obligation; an implementation-diverse checker matching across a golden vector, 18 mutations, reversed set orders, and 256 generated cases; and a Lean kernel-checked theorem over a handwritten model of the checker. None of those proves the Rust binary itself or the underlying SHA-256 hash function.

I also spent roughly 80 phases pushing Rust-to-Lean extraction through Aeneas. Extraction ran, logs piled up, and no complete kernel-checked witness emerged—every stretch ended in tooling, environment, or execution-protocol stops, each documented and cleaned up rather than swept aside. Walking away without the shiny theorem hurt for a week, and then I noticed what had quietly survived those 80 rounds: a fail-closed execution state machine, a 102-command source ledger, and an evidence pipeline structurally incapable of promoting a formal claim it hadn’t earned. Protocol discipline cost more than the math—and turned out to be the lasting gift.

Hardware attestation as a bridge, not the destination

On multi-party computation, the status is one sentence: I have no working MPC protocol to show. What I do have is hardware attestation, which addresses a slice of the same trust problem from another angle. Running our 20-scenario agent-maintenance suite inside a Google Confidential Space Intel TDX host produced a signed attestation covering image digest, source revision, and hardware security state. That shrinks who you have to trust, yet it is still a halfway house: the silicon vendor stays inside your trust circle.

Sitting quietly on my study shelf alongside it is a public homomorphic-encryption bounty artifact whose first edition had to be withdrawn after an extra commitment accidentally let attackers test plaintext guesses offline. I have no result on it; I keep it nearby as a gentle reminder that even polished cryptographic artifacts can leak an unintended oracle if nobody checks the side doors.

Listening to the quantum clock

Every signed agent receipt in my repositories leans on elliptic-curve cryptography, which means their durability lives on a countdown. Through ECDSA.Fail—a public, evaluator-verified research loop optimizing reversible secp256k1 point-addition circuits inside Shor’s algorithm—participants cut the optimized Q × T score by 86.1%, reaching 1.496 billion at the July arXiv cutoff, alongside a separately reported 1.259 billion coherent windowed figure. Because that coherent windowed variant is a per-call sensitivity model and neighboring papers use different interfaces and accounting rules, those numbers are not a full-Shor forecast or a clean ranking. Still, the directional message is unmistakable: cheaper circuits pull the planning horizon closer, which is a reason to prepare post-quantum migration with calm urgency rather than wait for a deadline.

Right beside that, I want to be transparent about two nearby realities. First, I have not yet implemented or benchmarked a post-quantum signature scheme myself; bringing our semantics-first adversarial mutation harness to post-quantum verification inside agent receipts remains an inviting road I haven’t started walking. Second, when we tested a quantum-adjacent idea in MeshQRC—a quantum reservoir computing lane for industrial anomaly detection, keeping live inference strictly classical—a tuned local QRC led on synthetic regimes yet trailed strong classical baselines on real server-machine and industrial-control proxies, so we deferred further remote QRC work. Quantum physics hands out no free lunch, and a good benchmark tells you so right away.

So if you walk out of this room looking for live zkVM leaderboards, working MPC, post-quantum libraries, or verified Rust binaries, you won’t find them here yet. What sits on the table is an honest ledger of the threat horizon, a semantics-first mutation framework waiting for real provers, and the willingness to name empty shelves plainly until real work fills them.
