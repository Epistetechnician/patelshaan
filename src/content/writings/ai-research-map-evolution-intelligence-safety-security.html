What We Know and Still Don't: A Research Map

This is the front door to a series of notes on six questions I have spent the past year on: whether AI systems can keep learning without losing themselves, what they represent inside, whether their agents can be held to account, and whether the infrastructure beneath them can be verified by the people who depend on it. I am publishing all of it, including the dead ends, because most of what I learned is what did not work, and that is the part other people most need.

The one rule behind everything

Every project here follows a single discipline. A result is bound to an exact state slice, a protocol, and a claim ceiling, from a design note at the bottom to an independently reproduced result at the top. A synthetic pass qualifies a harness and not a hypothesis. A local replay is not independent evidence. A model may propose evidence, but it may not approve it, widen its own capabilities, edit its own evaluator, or promote its own update.

Nearly all the work sits at the lowest rung of that ladder: bounded, local, and not independently replicated. When a post says "passed," read it with that ceiling attached. If you want the whole argument in one place, the flagship essay in the systems section ties these notes together.

The six directions

  1. Continual learning and alignment benchmarks. Can a model keep its competence and its values as it keeps learning? A synthetic harness passes, a matched-progress comparison fails, and the real-model series mostly ends in "no candidate."
  2. Hybrid synthetic-data systems. Synthetic data as a provenance-bound factory that is never allowed to certify itself, and the evaluation gates that make that enforceable.
  3. Mechanistic interpretability and nonlinguistic representations. One narrow measurement qualification, a long run of terminal negatives, and a result about why raw coordinates are the wrong unit for mechanism claims.
  4. Verifiable AI agents. A kernel in which an untrusted proposal acts only through evidence, admission, a single-use capability, and a sandbox, with 279 passing tests and one hardware-attested run.
  5. MPC, zero-knowledge proofs, and post-quantum cryptography. A semantic benchmark generator for ZK, a formal-evidence ladder that refuses to over-claim, and the quantum-circuit work behind ECDSA.fail. The thinnest area for built results, and the post says so.
  6. Decentralized compute and data infrastructure. Outcome protocols, replay-safe settlement, tamper-evident run logs, and data lineage as the primitives a community would need to verify work it did not do itself.

How the questions connect

Intelligence and evolution questions (does it keep learning, what is it representing) feed safety questions (does it still behave, and can we tell). Safety questions become security questions the moment an agent can act on someone's behalf, because then the issue is what an untrusted proposal can cause and who can prove what happened. And proofs are only as durable as the cryptography under them and as independent as the infrastructure that checks them. Each layer borrows the humility of the one before it.

What I would want a reader to take away

  • Terminal negative results were the main output. I kept them.
  • Process and tooling failures cost more phases than science failures. Recording them as stops, not results, kept the claims honest. One interpretability lane, for instance, was blocked outright because a pinned dependency had no build for the machine's architecture.
  • A fail-closed refusal to promote evidence is a feature, even when it makes the headline smaller.
  • Narrow positive claims are the right kind to make. None of mine has been promoted beyond its ceiling.
  • Measurement is usually the weakest link. Two graders disagreed by a factor of three about how safe the same outputs were.
