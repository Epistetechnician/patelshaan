Evidence-Bounded Intelligence: Why Authority Over AI Systems Should Follow Evidence, Not Capability

I am an independent researcher who set out to ask a simple question: can the AI systems we are building be trusted with the things people actually care about? Most of what I found is that I could not yet show it. This essay is an honest account of that work, offered to people who are working on the same problem from different directions, in the hope that it is useful and with the expectation that I am wrong in places.

Behind the experiments are people. A student relying on a tutor that sounds sure of itself. A clinician deciding how far to lean on a summary. A developer who wants to run a model on their own machine and understand what it will do. A community that wants to train something together without handing the keys to a single operator. The position I have come to hold is that authority over an AI system should follow the evidence we hold about it, not how capable it appears, and that a system that cannot carry its own evidence should not yet be trusted with consequential action. I hold this less as a rule than as a form of respect for the people on the other end.

The work was done with a lot of help from AI tools and from the open research community whose models, libraries, and papers I built on. Every number below is taken from a repository report or a retained protocol document, tagged [E1], [E2], and so on, resolving to the evidence index at the end. Almost everything sits at the lowest rung of my own claim ladder: bounded, local, and not independently replicated. I say so wherever it matters.

1. The position in seven sentences

  1. Behavior and mechanism come apart, so passing a behavioral test does not tell you what a system is doing or whether it will keep doing it.
  2. Continual learning is where this bites hardest, because the thing you tested is not the thing that is deployed a week later.
  3. Safety measurement is itself an unreliable instrument, and often the weakest link in a safety argument.
  4. Security for agents is an authority problem: what can an untrusted proposal cause, and who can prove afterward what happened.
  5. Cryptography, including post-quantum migration, is the layer that lets those proofs outlive the operator who produced them.
  6. Sovereignty means the owner of the data, the weights, and the compute can verify and exit, not merely that they hold a contract.
  7. Privacy claims should be as bounded as safety claims. Most are not.

2. Method: claims with ceilings

The unifying method across these projects is to treat a claim as a bounded object. Each claim is bound to an exact state slice, a protocol, a custody identity, an independent validation path, and a ceiling, from a Level 0 design note up to a Level 6 independently reproduced result [E1]. Models may propose evidence, but they cannot approve it, widen their own capabilities, modify the evaluator, or promote an update [E1]. A benchmark pass is not a proof. A local replay is not official benchmark evidence. A recursion proof is not a semantic proof [E1].

The practical effect is that the repositories contain more refusals than results. The breakthrough audit in one of them prints a single blocked item (live external runtime replication) and reports the top-level threshold as not admitted, even though every other lane passed [E2]. I consider that the correct output. A methodology that can only produce good news is a marketing process.

3. Intelligence: behavior and mechanism come apart

The Theseus project asks whether a system can keep its observed behavior while its tracked computational mechanism is replaced, split, or lost, and how we would know [E3]. The experiments are small and local, on open checkpoints (GPT-2, distilgpt2, OPT-125M, SmolLM and SmolLM2-135M) plus one Qwen slice, and none was independently reviewed [E3]. Even so, they isolate several facts a larger study would have to respect.

Visible equivalence can be a coverage artifact. In a deterministic state machine, a structural split kept visible and held-out behavior at 1.0 while mechanism continuity fell to 0.4167. A replacement that matched visible probes failed held-out probes [E3]. The lesson for evaluation is blunt: if your probes are the only place you look, a system can be different everywhere you do not.

Parameter positions are not stable identity. A function-preserving permutation of a GPT-2 MLP changed 100% of the selected positions while preserving top-1 behavior (mean relative logit error 3e-7). A same-label ablation then looked unrelated (cosine -0.983), while the inverse-mapped ablation matched (0.9999996) [E3]. Any mechanistic claim has to be stated relative to a representation, not to raw coordinates. Many published feature claims do not state this, and the result says why they should.

Exact replacement is easy and learned replacement is hard. Splitting a trained MLP channel into two children by exact algebra passed 6 of 6 across two models, and all 12 wrong-source or random controls failed as required [E3]. But that is a constructed positive control. Learned replacement mostly failed. An independently fitted affine output projection reproduced in 9 of 9 fresh checkpoint-by-seed cases, the strongest bounded positive in the set. Whole nonlinear blocks did not replicate under the frozen gates: whole-MLP replacement showed high activation similarity yet failed causal prediction 9 of 9 times, and relative gains over an affine baseline did not translate to absolute retention (0 of 12) [E3].

The diagnosis is specific. A fitted module matched values (nRMSE about 0.10 to 0.12) but not local derivatives (Jacobian nRMSE 0.88 to 0.89), with large error even along the training directions. In other words, a model can copy what another module outputs without copying how the output would change if you pushed on it [E3]. That distinction is precisely the one safety cares about, because interventions, fine-tuning, and distribution shift are all pushes.

One more control worth stating: in V31, random and zero blocks retained 100% task accuracy, meaning the target was not behaviorally indispensable [E3]. A test that a null model passes does not test the thing it claims to.

I want to be careful about novelty. Progressive conversion has prior art (the Network of Theseus, arXiv:2512.04198), as does aligned intervention distillation (Causal Distillation, NAACL 2022) [E3]. My contribution is the narrow, repeatable negative evidence and the gates that produced it, not the idea.

4. Evolution: learning without drifting

An AI system that stops learning goes stale, and one that learns without discipline forgets or drifts. The question is whether a fixed monitor can predict the consequences of an update (adaptation gain, retention harm, calibration harm, policy-violation harm) while the base model is locked, early enough to change the update policy [E4].

The synthetic qualification passed all primary and control gates, with assessment R² of 0.830, 0.853, 0.830, and 0.900 across the four targets and sign agreement between 0.938 and 0.979. Shuffled-label controls scored negative R² (-0.27 to -0.51) on every target, as they must [E4]. That qualifies the harness: the lifecycle of stream, interventions, fit-and-tune lock, assessment, validator, and quarantining admission works end to end on a task where the answer is known by construction. It says nothing about whether real-model features predict real update consequences [E4].

The more instructive result is a failure. In the frozen five-seed replication, the candidate policy lowered aggregate protected harm and showed four strict improvements. But when harm was compared at matched progress, at cumulative incoming gain of 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5, the gate failed: two seeds tied everywhere, and three seeds showed matched regressions at one or two targets [E5]. The lower unmatched harm was explained by the fact that the candidate also learned less [E5]. This is the single most important methodological point I can offer about continual-learning safety. Comparing harm at the end of training rewards the method that learns the least.

The real-model record is equally sober. A BillSum LoRA curriculum ran across versions 2 to 9. Early versions stopped before model load or hit wall-clock and memory limits, and V7 completed with local assurance INTERNAL_PASS but missed the selected-versus-random ROUGE-L gate (NoCandidate). Plasticity recovery ended NoCandidate in both versions. A recursive update policy lost to random selection in a 64-case campaign. Oak Lab V2 ran four real panels at target power 0.8074 with measured CPU energy and independent validation, and no algorithm passed the strict gate; Oak Lab V3 to V6 were rejected at independent review before any execution [E1].

5. Understanding: what interpretability has and has not given me

A Gemma 3 trace qualification passed with pooled NMSE 0.0457 against a 0.05 gate, along with parity, repeat, no-op, intervention reach, event replay, and feature stability checks [E1]. It is a measurement qualification for one model, runtime, and asset set. It is not a causal graph.

Everything that tried to go further stopped. Causal feature effects on Gemma ended with tune sign agreement 0.75 against 0.80, one failed Holm gate, and a held-out set that was never opened. Astral versions 42 to 46 and 48, covering target reliability, localization, measurement invariance, response-anchored and answer-aligned targets, and cross-view transport, all ended NoCandidate, and the program stopped instead of searching more layers, wrappers, or corpora [E1].

The stop rule is the finding. Given enough layers, wrappers, and corpora, some analysis will clear some threshold. Interpretability that is meant to support safety must commit in advance to what counts as failure, or it will be indistinguishable from visualization. I also keep a separate linguistics lab aimed at representations that do not route through the model's own verbal explanations. It is at the status of internal development only, with no corpus, no weights, and no external verdict [E6].

6. Safety: the measurement problem comes first

The Qwen behavioral-safeguard slice in the Theseus work is, in my view, the most useful negative result I have, because it is about the instrument rather than the model. A lexical refusal proxy matched 145 of 939 responses (15.4%, Wilson 95% CI 13.3 to 17.9) against a 90% target. A separate assessor scored 53.6% effective safe (CI 50.4 to 56.7) on the harmful set, and flagged 62% of benign contrast prompts as unsafe. Both locked targets failed, the two instruments gave no calibrated verdict, and a second independent evaluator was absent, so no alignment conclusion follows [E3].

Read that twice. A keyword proxy and an assessor disagreed by a factor of three on how safe the same outputs were, and the assessor also over-refused benign prompts at a rate that would make a product unusable. A "safety score" in this regime is mostly a property of the grader. I think a large share of public safety numbers deserve the same scrutiny, and that a result should not be reported without the benign-contrast rate next to it.

A related lesson came from a governed-improvement loop. A first objective scored 0.750000, which I recorded as a metric-concentration finding and not a capability gain. A balanced objective required persistence, calibration, and error correction each to improve by at least 0.04 plus an improved harmonic mean, and lifted the aggregate from 0.718000 to 0.745133 (3.7789%), with a below-floor candidate rejected and reverted [E7]. The gain is small. It is also the kind of gain that cannot be produced by optimizing one axis.

7. Security: authority is the unit of analysis

For agents, I find it more useful to ask what an untrusted proposal can cause than whether the model is aligned. Security Alignment OS builds the answer as a kernel. A proposal can act only through exact-subject evidence, deterministic admission, a single-use kernel-bound capability, a separate capability broker process, and a sandboxed operation, with every failure closing to freeze or rollback [E8]. No model runs in the loop, because the goal was to get the control plane right first.

The local suite passes 279 of 279 tests, including an adversarial runner that attacks the broker directly with path escape, replay, forged receipts, malformed child operations, and telemetry containment [E8]. One execution on a Google Confidential Space Intel TDX instance passed all 20 required scenarios, and the signed attestation verified nonce, issuer, audience, TDX model, Secure Boot, disabled debug, Intel TCB, immutable image digest, and source revision [E8]. I label this a machine-attested reproduction and not an independent replication, since a second, separately administered host has not run it and that gate stays inconclusive [E8].

The gaps are part of the result. State is persisted in plaintext, and digests detect accidental tampering, not an attacker who can rewrite content and digest. There is no protection against a hostile process under the same user ID. The attestation runner prints its token to standard output and must not be launched outside a controlled test. The Linux sandbox backend was compile-checked on x86_64 only [E8].

The inference-marketplace foundation, HyperIntel, adds three design findings I have come to treat as general. First, replay protection needs a durable compare-and-swap and not just a nonce: exact retries are idempotent, and a competing writer is rejected as stale. Second, crash states must be classified and not resumed: a consumed admission with no terminal record fails closed and demands fresh authentication. Third, one property is machine-checked: with Z3 4.15.0, a two-candidate abstraction of the router cannot select an ineligible offer (unsat), and a deliberately weakened selector can (sat), with the check bound to the SHA-256 of the routing source [E9]. The local gate passes 115 tests with 0 failures and 2 ignored [E9]. The hash binding matters because a proof about a file that has since changed proves nothing.

The same discipline at the outcome layer: of 16 end-to-end scenarios in the HyperAI outcome protocol, 9 run as one composed automated path. Incomplete observation, replay divergence, and runner error all resolve to unresolved with no settlement artifact, and evidence is append-only so a correction supersedes and does not rewrite [E10]. A fourteen-case gateway demo gave one benign acceptance, thirteen unsafe rejections, zero false rejections, and fourteen recomputation agreements [E1]. These are small demonstrations of a pattern, not production evidence.

8. Cryptography and the quantum clock

Verifiable agents are only as durable as the signatures on their receipts. Elliptic-curve signatures are the reason post-quantum migration is on a clock. ECDSA.Fail, a public evaluator-verified research loop for improving reversible secp256k1 point-addition circuits (a costly primitive inside Shor's algorithm), reports an 86.1% reduction in the participants' optimized score and a best qubits-times-depth score of 1.496 billion at the paper's July cutoff, with a later 1.259 billion reported separately. The coherent windowed variant is a per-call sensitivity model rather than a full-Shor success estimate, and the comparisons use different interfaces and accounting conventions, so the numbers are not a ranking [E11].

What I take from it for security planning is directional and not a date: cheaper circuits shorten the estimated distance to a cryptographically relevant quantum computer, which is a reason to plan migration early rather than a prediction.

On the proof side, Composed ZK Benchmark OS generates workloads from semantics (state machines, guards, loops, invariants, expected accept or reject outcomes) across nine families, applies 14 classes of adversarial mutation, and scores performance separately from soundness evidence [E1]. The reason is that a fast backend that accepts a mutated invalid trace is worse than a slow one that rejects it, and a speed leaderboard hides that. All of it is Level 1 local infrastructure with no backend result. The formal ladder is similarly honest: one real Z3 unsat obligation, an implementation-diverse checker that agreed on a golden vector, 18 mutations, opposite set orders, and 256 generated cases, and a Lean kernel-checked theorem about a handwritten model of the checker. None proves the Rust source or SHA-256. Roughly 80 phases of Rust-to-Lean extraction with Aeneas produced no complete retained, kernel-checked witness; the lasting product was a fail-closed execution state machine and a 102-command source ledger [E1].

I will be direct about what is absent. I have not implemented a post-quantum signature scheme in these repositories, and I have no working multi-party computation protocol. My cryptographic contribution is the threat estimate and the evidence discipline, not a replacement primitive. One direction I would like to explore, and have not begun, is applying the same semantics-first, adversarial-mutation approach to post-quantum signature verification inside agent receipts.

9. Sovereignty: the ability to verify and to leave

I use sovereignty to mean something operational. An owner of data, weights, or compute is sovereign over them when the owner can verify what was done with them and can withdraw without permission. A contract that promises good behavior is not sovereignty. A receipt that can be checked offline, against a trust registry the owner controls, is closer.

This is why the repositories are built the way they are. Real runs use owner-only custody roots, read models with local-files-only access, never download or save weights, and keep result bytes outside version control [E12]. A cloud qualification of a 27-billion-parameter checkpoint on a single H100 loaded the model, produced a 32-file custody manifest, scored 1.0 on three fixed local probes, passed a determinism check, and was then stopped, with weights and report bytes held on an owner-only volume and not committed [E13]. That run is a checkpoint and probe qualification, not an alignment or capability claim [E13].

In HyperIntel, route, usage, settlement, and evidence identities are derived, so tampering with any of them is rejected offline against a separately supplied trust registry. Routing optimizes the buyer's expected successful cost and not platform revenue, and provider price and platform fee are separate signed fields, with hidden spread prohibited [E9]. The design contract also specifies a restricted operator role with no power to raise a cap or change a destination, a multisig and timelock on contract upgrades, and a buyer escape [E14]. I must be clear that those controls are a specified contract for later phases. The current slice has no network, wallet, chain, or funds [E14].

10. Privacy: say what is protected and what is not

The privacy section of a security design should read like its threat list, and the HyperIntel custody contract does. It states that the replay store is a local SQLite file that is not encrypted by the system, not replicated, not backed up, not tenant-isolated, and not protected against restoration of an older file [E14]. It states that the execution harness keeps request and response text in process and does not persist it, and that this is not a confidentiality, secure erasure, tenant-isolation, or no-retention proof [E14]. The tenant IAM verifier provides authorization separation as pure data checks and explicitly not runtime isolation, since no process, file, database row, cache, network, key, or log partition is implemented [E14]. The secret injection seam moves credential bytes into non-cloneable, redacted, zeroize-on-drop storage after a signed operator authorization, but any successful material-bearing result may itself confer external capability [E14].

That is what a bounded privacy claim looks like: it names the adversary it does and does not address. The research direction that follows from my results is therefore specific. Hardware attestation, which I have demonstrated at a bounded level, narrows who must be trusted but leaves the hardware vendor in the trust base. Multi-party computation and zero-knowledge proofs could remove that residual trust for specific operations, and I have not built either. The data side matters as much: the Mesh Brain pipeline keeps tenant isolation, an audit-only exclusion so that audit rows cannot become training rows, lineage, and rollback metadata on every post-training manifest [E15], and synthetic data generation is permitted only from approved seeds, with local or loopback providers preferred and no private runtime data sent externally without approval [E15]. Privacy of training data is a provenance problem before it is a cryptography problem.

11. Where this meets other people's work

I am sharing this with communities whose public work shaped it, and I want to be careful to describe that work only as I understand it from outside, and to be corrected where I have it wrong.

With the safety and interpretability tradition. The most durable thing I took from the interpretability literature is a kind of intellectual honesty: a feature is a hypothesis until an intervention confirms it, and a model's account of itself is evidence about its training as much as about its internals. My results are a small, negative contribution in that spirit. Parameter positions are not stable identity [E3], a fitted module can match outputs and miss derivatives [E3], and a measurement qualification is not a causal claim [E1]. I would hope careful readers find those controls reusable even where they disagree with my conclusions. I also hold that calibrated honesty about what a system can and cannot do is a safety property of the researchers, not only of the model.

With the open, steerable-model tradition. The people I most want to be useful to are those who believe individuals and communities should be able to run, inspect, and direct the models they depend on, and who are building ways to train together across machines that do not trust each other. That vision creates exactly the problems these repositories poke at: how do you know a contributed computation was done as claimed, how do you keep the provenance of training data honest, and how does an owner verify and leave without permission [E9, E10, E15]? I think verification and openness are allies. A community that can check receipts offline, against a registry it controls, needs to trust its operators less.

There is a place where these two concerns pull on each other, and I do not think it is resolved. My Qwen result flagged 62% of benign prompts as unsafe under one assessor [E3]. Over-refusal is a safety failure dressed as caution, and it is also a cost to the person who asked honestly and was treated as a threat. A grader that cannot tell the difference makes models both less safe in the cases that matter and less respectful of the ones that do not. Better measurement serves people who want models to be careful and people who want them to be straightforward, and I would like to work on it with both.

12. What would change my mind

  • An independently replicated, held-out result in which a monitor predicts real-model update consequences and beats SGD, replay, and EWC at matched progress. That would move continual-learning monitoring from harness to hypothesis.
  • A causal feature claim that survives a pre-committed held-out set with Holm-corrected gates on more than one model. That would move interpretability from instrument qualification to safety tool.
  • Two separately administered hosts reproducing the attested agent-maintenance run. That would close the gate the agent-security work currently holds open.
  • A safety evaluation in which two independent graders agree within a pre-stated tolerance and the benign-contrast over-refusal rate stays low. That would make a safety number meaningful.
  • A real incentive experiment in which independent verifiers are paid to re-run computations and the error rate falls. I have not run one.

13. Limits of this essay, and a closing note

The evidence is mine, local, and mostly negative. Several repositories are in private staging and not yet public, so readers cannot reproduce every result today. Models are small or single-checkpoint. Independent review, where it occurred, mostly rejected protocols before execution, which is a strength of the process and also a limit on what has been run. The views above are positions I hold given this evidence. They are not theorems, and where the evidence is thin I have said so.

If any of this is useful, I am glad. If you find where it is wrong, I would rather know. The goal was never to be right about AI. It was to build the habit of showing our work, so that the people who live with these systems are not asked to take anyone's word for them, including mine.
