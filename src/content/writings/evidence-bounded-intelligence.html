Evidence-Bounded Intelligence: Why Authority Over AI Systems Should Follow Evidence, Not Capability

Somewhere right now, a student is reading an answer from a tutor that sounds completely sure of itself, a clinician is deciding how much of a patient’s chart summary to lean on during a twelve-hour shift, and a small team is wondering whether they can run a capable model on their own hardware without handing their future to a distant operator. Every experiment I ran over the past year began inside that ordinary human scene—and with a simple, uncomfortable question: do we actually hold enough evidence to ask those people to trust the systems we are building?

Honesty forces a quieter answer than most of this field prefers: most of the time, I could not yet show that we do. That realization did not make me cynical about AI. It gave me a firmer compass. Watching a model speak fluently, pass benchmarks, or write clean code feels remarkably like witnessing understanding, and our minds are wired to extend authority to fluency. Yet authority over consequential actions belongs to the evidence we actually hold about a system, never to the aura of how capable it appears. When a system cannot carry inspectable evidence for what it is about to do—and for what it has already done—withholding authority is an act of care for the people living downstream of our software.

Everything that follows grew out of open models, open libraries, generous public research, and a great deal of collaboration with AI tools themselves. I want to notice that irony out loud before a reader does: I am arguing for strict epistemic ceilings on AI while using AI daily to build and stress-test the work. That tension is precisely why every claim below carries a citation tag—[E1] through [E16]—pointing back to a concrete repository report or retained protocol in the evidence index. Nearly everything sits near the base of my own evidence ladder: bounded, local, and waiting for independent eyes. If you spot a seam where my reasoning slips, telling me is the best gift you can offer the work.

The habit of drawing ceilings before running the code

Before touching a single neural network, I had to confront my own easiest flaw as a researcher: the quiet pull to narrate a narrow success as a general breakthrough. When you spend weeks wiring a pipeline and a green checkmark finally appears on your terminal, your brain immediately starts writing the grand story. So I built a counter-habit into the repositories themselves. Every claim is treated as a bounded object tied to an exact state slice, a frozen protocol, a custody identity, a validation path, and an explicit ceiling—climbing from a Level 0 design note up to a Level 6 independently reproduced result [E1]. Inside that contract, a model may propose hypotheses or artifacts, yet it can never certify its own evidence, widen its own permissions, edit its own evaluator, or promote its own update [E1].

Living inside that rule changes the texture of a portfolio. You end up publishing far more refusals than victories. In Recoverable Ghost States, for instance, an automated breakthrough audit clears every internal lane and still refuses to admit the top-level threshold because one single gate—live external runtime replication—remains blocked [E2]. Watching a script print that refusal felt strangely grounding. A workflow incapable of telling you "not yet" has quietly stopped being science and turned into sales.

When behavior smiles while mechanism drifts

The oldest puzzle I kept returning to is the Ship of Theseus, carried inside a transformer. In the Theseus experiments—small, local studies over open checkpoints including GPT-2, distilgpt2, OPT-125M, SmolLM, SmolLM2-135M, and one Qwen slice [E3]—I wanted to feel firsthand how far observed behavior can travel without the underlying mechanism coming along for the ride.

Even in a deterministic state machine where nothing is hidden, a structural split kept visible and held-out behavior pegged at 1.0 while mechanism continuity fell to 0.4167; a replacement that matched every visible probe stumbled the moment held-out probes arrived [E3]. Move into real weights and the vertigo sharpens. Permuting the hidden units of a GPT-2 MLP moved 100% of the selected parameter positions while leaving top-1 behavior virtually untouched (mean relative logit error of 3e-7). When I ran the exact same ablation under the original coordinate labels, the effect looked inverted (cosine −0.983); once I mapped through the inverse permutation, it snapped back to 0.9999996 [E3]. Raw neuron indices are costumes, not identities. Any claim that "unit 417 causes behavior X" only means something relative to a chosen representation, and without a permutation control, a feature that appears to vanish between checkpoints may simply have changed seats.

Trying to replace learned blocks drove the lesson home. Exact algebraic splitting of a trained MLP channel passed 6 of 6 across two models while all 12 wrong-source and random controls failed on cue—a clean positive control, constructed by hand [E3]. Learned replacement was another story. An independently fitted affine output projection reproduced cleanly across 9 of 9 fresh checkpoint-by-seed cases, the sturdiest narrow positive in the series. Whole nonlinear MLP blocks, however, looked seductively similar in activation space and then failed causal prediction 9 of 9 times, with relative gains over affine baselines producing 0 of 12 absolute retention passes [E3].

The post-mortem revealed a geometric subtlety that changed how I think about safety. The fitted module learned where the original function sat—value nRMSE hovered around 0.10 to 0.12—while missing how it bent, with local Jacobian nRMSE stuck at 0.88 to 0.89 even along the training directions [E3]. You can imitate what a module says at rest without sharing how it reacts when pushed. Yet fine-tuning, distribution shift, and human edge cases are all pushes. And in one humbling control (V31), dropping in random and zero blocks preserved 100% task accuracy—meaning the target wasn’t even indispensable to the benchmark [E3]. Prior work already mapped progressive conversion (Network of Theseus, arXiv:2512.04198) and causal distillation (NAACL 2022) [E3]; what my notebook adds is a set of sharp negative controls reminding us how easily a flattering probe hides a hollow interior.

Learning without losing who you promised to be

That fracture between surface and interior hurts most in continual learning, because a model that adapts after deployment stops being the artifact you certified last Tuesday. Anyone who has watched a person or an institution evolve knows the delicacy of this tension: refuse to change and you ossify; change without roots and you drift into someone unrecognizable. I wanted to know whether a locked causal-feature monitor could forecast four consequences of an update before it ships—adaptation gain, retention harm, calibration harm, and policy-violation harm—well enough to steer the update policy itself [E4].

On a synthetic qualification task where truth is known by construction, the harness sang: assessment R² reached 0.830, 0.853, 0.830, and 0.900 across the four targets, sign agreement sat between 0.938 and 0.979, and shuffled-label controls sank properly into negative R² (−0.27 to −0.51) [E4]. It would have been tempting to celebrate there. Instead, that run only proved the plumbing works end to end—stream, interventions, fit-and-tune lock, validator, and quarantining admission kernel—not that real neural features predict real drift [E4].

The moment that actually reshaped my thinking came from a failure. In a frozen five-seed replication, a candidate policy reduced aggregate protected harm across four seeds and looked like a clear win. Then I forced an equal-footing comparison: measure harm at matched cumulative learning progress (incoming gain of 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5), interpolating strictly between observed checkpoints. The advantage evaporated. Two seeds tied everywhere; three regressed at one or more targets [E5]. The candidate had looked "safer" mainly because it had learned less [E5]. Once you see that trap, you spot it everywhere in continual learning: grading harm at the finish line quietly crowns whichever algorithm stayed timid.

Real models were even less forgiving. Across BillSum LoRA curriculum versions V2 through V9, early runs tripped over model loads, wall clocks, and memory ceilings before V7 finished with local assurance INTERNAL_PASS yet missed its selected-versus-random ROUGE-L bar (NoCandidate). Plasticity recovery closed as NoCandidate twice. A recursive update policy lost to random selection across 64 cases. Oak Lab V2 evaluated four real panels at target power 0.8074 with measured CPU energy and passed nothing; protocols V3 through V6 were stopped at independent review before spending a single compute cycle [E1]. Keeping those stops on the page respects the next researcher’s time.

When the ruler bends in your hand

The same humility returned when I turned to interpretability and safety grading. A Gemma 3 trace qualification cleared every check with pooled NMSE of 0.0457 against a 0.05 gate—parity, repeatability, no-op, intervention reach, replay, and stability all green [E1]. That qualified one instrument on one stack. Every attempt to climb from instrument check to causal claim hit a pre-committed stop: causal feature effects on Gemma fell short at 0.75 tune sign agreement against an 0.80 bar (leaving the held-out split sealed), and Astral versions V42 through V46 and V48 all closed NoCandidate without fishing across extra layers or corpora [E1]. Beside those runs sits a separate linguistics lab aimed at representations that do not route through a model’s own verbal self-reports—still strictly in internal development, with no corpus, no weights, and no external verdict [E6].

Yet the sharpest wake-up call arrived in the Qwen behavioral-safeguard slice, where the surprise had less to do with the model than with our thermometers. On the exact same 939 responses, a lexical refusal proxy marked 145 safe (15.4%, Wilson 95% CI 13.3–17.9%) against a 90% target, while a separate assessor scored 53.6% effective safe (CI 50.4–56.7%) on the harmful set—and simultaneously flagged 62% of completely benign contrast prompts as unsafe [E3]. Pause and sit with that number. Two graders looked at identical words and disagreed more than threefold, while the stricter judge treated six out of ten honest, harmless questions like threats.

Over-refusal is easy to brush aside as harmless caution until you remember the human sitting across the screen. A student asking a delicate medical or historical question who gets scolded by a miscalibrated filter learns that the system neither understands them nor respects their agency. A safety score reported without its benign-contrast rate hides half the moral picture. Even inside a governed-improvement loop, an early single-metric objective hit 0.750000 by concentrating on the easiest axis; switching to a balanced objective requiring persistence, calibration, and error correction to each rise by at least 0.04 alongside an improved harmonic mean lifted the aggregate more modestly from 0.718000 to 0.745133 (+3.7789%), rejecting and reverting a below-floor candidate along the way [E7]. Real progress rarely looks theatrical.

Authority, cryptography, and the right to walk away

Once an AI system can touch files, move funds, or message people on someone’s behalf, hoping a clever system prompt holds under pressure stops being fair to anyone affected. So in Security Alignment OS, I stripped the model out altogether to get the constitutional bones right first: an untrusted proposal can only act through exact-subject evidence, deterministic admission, a single-use kernel-bound capability, an isolated capability broker process, and a sandboxed operation that freezes or rolls back on any fault [E8].

Locally, 279 of 279 tests pass, including an adversarial runner that hammers the broker with path escapes, replays, forged receipts, malformed child operations, and telemetry leaks [E8]. On a Google Confidential Space Intel TDX host, all 20 required scenarios passed under a signed hardware attestation verifying nonce, issuer, audience, TDX model, Secure Boot, disabled debug, Intel TCB, immutable image digest, and source revision—a machine-attested reproduction, though still awaiting a second independently administered host [E8]. And the unfinished seams stay visible in plain daylight: persisted state is plaintext (catching accidents rather than a local attacker who rewrites content and digest together), a hostile process sharing the same user ID is outside current isolation, the attestation runner prints tokens to standard output and belongs strictly in controlled tests, and the Linux sandbox backend has only been compile-checked on x86_64 [E8].

Building the HyperIntel inference-marketplace prototype added three engineering instincts that travel everywhere: protect against replay with durable compare-and-swap transitions rather than bare nonces so competing writers fail cleanly as stale; classify crash states rather than silently resuming a consumed admission that lacks a terminal record; and machine-check narrow invariants bound directly to the SHA-256 of the source file—here, a Z3 4.15.0 proof confirming a two-candidate router abstraction cannot pick an ineligible offer (unsat) while a deliberately weakened copy can (sat), backed by 115 passing tests (0 failed, 2 ignored) [E9]. The HyperAI outcome protocol carries that same fail-closed grain into computation claims—9 of 16 end-to-end scenarios composed in one automated path where incomplete observations or replay divergence settle nothing and append-only logs supersede rather than erase—next to a small fourteen-case gateway demo yielding one benign acceptance, thirteen unsafe rejections, and zero false rejections [E10, E1].

Yet receipts only outlive their issuer as long as the cryptography underneath them holds. Working on ECDSA.Fail—a public, evaluator-verified research loop optimizing reversible secp256k1 point-addition circuits inside Shor’s algorithm—sharpened that horizon: participants cut the optimized qubits-times-depth score by 86.1% down to 1.496 billion at the July paper cutoff, with a separate coherent windowed variant later reaching 1.259 billion (a per-call sensitivity model across differing accounting conventions, not a full-Shor forecast or leaderboard) [E11]. That gives us direction rather than a calendar date: prepare post-quantum migration early. Meanwhile, Composed ZK Benchmark OS generates semantic workloads across nine families and 14 adversarial mutation classes, scoring soundness separately from speed so a fast prover that accepts a forged trace can never look heroic—all honest Level 1 local infrastructure alongside one Z3 digest-binding check, an implementation-diverse checker matching across 256 generated cases, a Lean theorem over a handwritten model, and roughly 80 sobering phases of Aeneas Rust-to-Lean extraction that yielded a fail-closed execution state machine and a 102-command source ledger rather than a full Rust witness [E1]. I have built neither a post-quantum signature implementation nor a working MPC protocol yet; applying semantics-first adversarial mutation to post-quantum agent receipts is a road still waiting ahead.

Sovereignty and privacy as lived relations

People throw around the word sovereignty until it sounds like a slogan. In practice it feels concrete and personal: can the owner of data, weights, or compute verify what happened to them and leave without asking permission? That conviction shaped how our runs touch files—owner-only custody roots, local-files-only reads, raw outputs kept out of version control, and an H100 qualification of a 27-billion-parameter checkpoint that produced a 32-file custody manifest, cleared three fixed probes deterministically, and stopped right there without pretending to be an alignment finding [E12, E13]. In HyperIntel, derived identities let a buyer verify routing, usage, and settlement offline against their own trust registry with provider price and platform fee signed separately so hidden spreads cannot hide, while a future-phase economics contract specifies restricted operator powers, timelocked multisigs, and buyer exits—clearly marked as specification rather than live funds [E9, E14].

Privacy deserves that same unflinching candor. HyperIntel’s custody contract reads like a frank threat inventory: the local SQLite replay store is unencrypted, unreplicated, and vulnerable to rollback; the in-process harness makes no secure-erasure or confidentiality promise; the tenant IAM verifier checks pure authorization data rather than kernel-level isolation; and secret injection uses redacted, zeroize-on-drop memory while noting that a material-bearing output can still carry external capability [E14]. Hardware attestation narrows whom you must trust while leaving the silicon vendor inside the circle; MPC and zero-knowledge proofs could tighten that further once built. Upstream, Mesh Brain enforces tenant boundaries, rollback metadata, and strict audit-only exclusions so evaluation rows cannot quietly slip into training sets, generating synthetic rows strictly from approved seeds on local or loopback endpoints [E15]. Long before privacy becomes cryptography, it is basic respect for provenance.

Two communities pulling on the same rope

Writing this in public means standing between two traditions I admire deeply. From the safety and interpretability community, I inherited the discipline that a feature is a hypothesis until an intervention confirms it, and that calibrated honesty is a property of the human researcher before it is a property of the model. From the open, steerable-model community, I inherited the conviction that individuals and neighborhoods should be able to run, inspect, and direct the intelligence woven into their lives rather than renting permission from a single gatekeeper [E9, E10, E15].

Those two instincts occasionally collide, and the 62% benign over-refusal rate in my own Qwen slice sits right on the fault line [E3]. A blunt grader harms both sides at once: it fails to catch real danger while treating ordinary, curious people like adversaries. Better measurement isn’t a compromise between safety and freedom—it is the common ground that lets us protect people without infantilizing them.

Since these first drafts were assembled, a November 2026 wave of seven public repositories pushed several of these threads from concept to pre-registered receipt [E16]—from Holdout Ledger’s 0 of 59,200 adaptive certificate violations and FormaSec’s 0 of 552 hostile admissions across Python, Rust, and Lean 4, to Caduceus’s governed skill lifts, Playbox’s H100 memorizer catch, Mechanistic Interpretability Unified’s Qwen3 replication alongside planted-flaw and J-space equivalence nulls, Temporal Grid Safety’s benign-drift refusal retention, and HSI Lab’s recomputable synthetic-data packets.

What would change my mind

Holding a position honestly means naming in advance what would move you off it. Five pieces of evidence would shift my view substantially:

  • An independently replicated, held-out study where an update monitor forecasts real-model consequences and outperforms SGD, replay, and EWC at matched learning progress.
  • A causal feature claim that survives a pre-committed held-out split under Holm-corrected gates across more than one model family, turning instrument checks into a genuine safety handle.
  • Two separately administered confidential-compute hosts independently reproducing the attested agent-maintenance loop.
  • A safety benchmark where two independent evaluators agree within a declared tolerance while keeping benign-contrast over-refusal genuinely low.
  • A live economic experiment where paying independent verifiers to recompute work demonstrably drives error rates down.

Look closely at the edges of everything written here: much of the evidence is local, single-author, small-scale, or negative, and several early staging repositories are still working their way toward public release. That limitation is part of the invitation. The point was never to stand on a stage and declare myself right about AI. It was to practice showing our work in full view—cracks, dead ends, and all—so the people who have to live with these systems are never asked to take anyone’s word for them, least of all mine.
