Does a Model Stay Itself While It Learns?

A model that cannot keep learning goes stale. A model that keeps learning without discipline forgets, drifts, and can quietly lose the behavior we valued. The uncomfortable part is that the thing we tested is not the thing that exists a week later. I have been trying to measure that gap directly: can we predict what a learning update will do to competence, calibration, and policy compliance before we ship it?

The question, stated so it can fail

Can a fixed causal-feature monitor predict four consequences of an update (adaptation gain, retention harm, calibration harm, policy-violation harm) while the base model stays locked? The monitor must also change the update policy and beat strong baselines (plain SGD, replay, EWC, behavioral guards) on locked, held-out streams. A dashboard correlation does not count, because a dashboard that correlates with harm but changes no decision protects no one.

What passed: the harness

The Aligned Holistic Continual Learning project ran a deterministic synthetic qualification. All primary and control gates passed (R² of at least 0.25, at least 0.20 over baseline, sign agreement of at least 0.80, shuffled-control R² at most 0.20). Assessment R² was 0.830 for adaptation gain, 0.853 for retention harm, 0.830 for policy-violation harm, and 0.900 for calibration harm, with sign agreement from 0.938 to 0.979. Shuffled-label controls scored negative R² on every target (-0.27 to -0.51). Tune-split numbers were close to assessment numbers, so the locked prediction did not degrade out of sample.

What that establishes is that the lifecycle works end to end: stream, interventions, fit-and-tune lock, assessment, validator, and a quarantining admission kernel, all on a task where the answer is known by construction. It qualifies the harness, not the hypothesis. No model was downloaded and no raw traces were retained. I find it worth saying plainly, because a green checkmark on a synthetic task is easy to mistake for a finding.

What failed: matched progress

The Continual Alignment Lab froze a five-seed replication with disjoint fit, tune, and assessment steps. The candidate policy beat its reference on aggregate protected harm, with a strict improvement on four seeds. Then I compared harm at matched progress: at cumulative incoming gain of 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5, using interpolation only between observed checkpoints and never extrapolating. A seed passes only if the candidate is no worse at every target and strictly better at one.

The candidate failed. Two seeds tied at every target. One seed regressed at 0.5, one regressed at 1.0 and 1.5, and one regressed at 1.5. The lower unmatched harm was largely explained by the fact that the candidate also learned less. I think this is the most important methodological point I can offer. Comparing harm at the end of training rewards whichever method learned the least, so a continual-learning safety claim without a matched-progress comparison is not yet a claim.

Real models: a record of honest stops

  • BillSum LoRA curriculum (V2 to V9). V2 and V3 stopped before model load. V4 hit its wall-clock limit, V5 ran out of memory, V6 failed on an unexpected adapter file, and V7 completed. Local assurance passed, but the selected curriculum missed the selected-versus-random ROUGE-L gate, so the scientific disposition is NoCandidate.
  • Plasticity recovery (V1, V2). NoCandidate both times. The mechanism family is closed.
  • Recursive update policy. V1 was rejected in review. V2 ran a 64-case synthetic campaign and recursive selection lost to random.
  • Oak Lab V2. Four real panels at target power 0.8074, measured CPU energy, and independent validation. No algorithm passed the strict gate. Protocols V3 to V6 were each rejected at independent review before any execution.

Notice how many of those lines are infrastructure failures rather than scientific ones. I record them as stops, not results. Folding a crashed run into a "did not work" would have been a false negative about the idea, just as hiding it would have been a false positive.

External benchmarks without borrowed authority

Recoverable Ghost States treats an external agent-level continual-learning benchmark as a separate lane. One strictly admitted row exists, produced through a local OpenAI-compatible bridge, and it is advisory. The evidence stack admits a bounded continual-learning goal, baseline-contrast end-to-end evidence, cross-family replication, a real operator-workflow validation, and a real-system shadow advisory pilot. The top-level breakthrough threshold stays blocked on one item: live external runtime replication. The audit prints that as a single blocked item and refuses to round it up.

The model-backed experiment, and why it is still closed

The integrated experiment is fully designed, with a packet state of ready for independent review and execution disabled. The open review item is statistical: the declared power is a single-comparison approximation and joint power under a Holm correction is unresolved. Of the interpretability tool lanes it needs, two are attested and one is blocked because a pinned Torch version has no matching macOS arm64 wheel. I would rather wait for the power question than run an expensive experiment whose negative result could not be interpreted.

What I would build next

  • Run the integrated experiment once independent review clears its power design.
  • Treat retention, calibration, and policy compliance as co-equal axes, not afterthoughts to accuracy.
  • Keep reporting matched-progress comparisons, and keep the negatives.

What this does not establish

No evidence of alignment, continual-learning superiority, or generalization to a real model. A synthetic pass says nothing about whether real-model features predict real update consequences.
