Epistemic Engineering: Instruments for Knowing Whether a Claim, Self-Improvement, or Coordination State Is Real

For four centuries, scientific and institutional trust relied on an unspoken rate limit: generating a plausible claim was expensive enough that human peers could still inspect it. That rate limit just broke. Autonomous AI agents now produce code patches, mechanistic hypotheses, weight updates, and synthetic datasets at machine speed; federated prediction venues settle multi-leg claims across asynchronous oracles and blockchains; automated pipelines label thousands of historical texts overnight. When claim generation approaches zero marginal cost while human attention stays fixed, the scarce resource is no longer compute or cleverness—it is a small, cheap, mechanical instrument for knowing whether an empirical claim, an autonomous self-improvement, or a multi-party coordination state is real without trusting its producer.

1. Why Five Existing Silos Leave a Structural Gap

Pieces of the verification problem sit marooned inside five separate disciplines that rarely share tools or forums:

  • ML & AI Evaluations build leaderboards and hill-climbs that silently collapse under adaptive querying, dev-set memorization, and reward hacking.
  • Security & Formal Methods prove deterministic isolation kernels in Rust, Lean, and Kani while stopping short of noisy empirical claims and statistical power.
  • Anytime Statistics & Information Theory prove martingale e-processes and Minimum Description Length (MDL) bounds that rarely ship inside OS-custodied agent brokers.
  • Market Microstructure & Crypto track spreads and latency without unified formal residuals across asynchronous venue, oracle, and settlement finality clocks.
  • Interpretability & Computational Humanities hunt for internal circuits and cultural patterns without systematically auditing whether their own instruments distinguish real signal from apophenia.

Between those five silos sits Epistemic Engineering, anchored quantitatively by Adversarial Metrology: the craft of building small, tamper-evident measurement and verification instruments that stay valid when the system being measured adapts, drifts, hides behind asynchronous clocks, or games its evaluator.
