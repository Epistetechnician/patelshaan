Sacred Economics in Cryptographic Systems

2024 • Sacred Economics & Alternative Systems

Every public-goods funding season leaves me holding two incompatible worlds in the same afternoon. On one side are exhausted, devoted open-source maintainers keeping shared digital infrastructure standing on modest grants, sustained by strangers pooling small contributions across continents. Moments later, the wider crypto feed roars back to life with zero-sum extraction, status theater, and predatory token launches dressed up in the vocabulary of liberation.

That ache refuses to fade because I still believe cryptographic systems can carry moral imagination alongside financial plumbing. Yet wallets, signatures, and smart contracts carry no automatic grace of their own. Swap banks for protocols while keeping the same inner grammar of scarcity, hoarding, and exit over care, and you end up building gleaming gated towers atop a commons nobody knows how to tend.

The quiet emotional weather inside money

Reading Charles Eisenstein’s Sacred Economics gave language to a hunch I had felt for years without quite naming it: money behaves less like neutral software plumbing and more like weather. It teaches us, subtly and relentlessly, what feels smart, what feels foolish, whom we owe gratitude, and whom we can safely treat as a stranger.

"Sacred Economics traces the history of money from ancient gift economies to modern capitalism, revealing how the money system has contributed to alienation, competition, and scarcity, destroyed community, and necessitated endless growth."
— Sacred Economics

Think about the habits of relating encoded into a token design. An architecture that crowns whoever hoards longest tells one story about human worth; an architecture that rewards circulation, stewardship, and showing up when hype leaves tells another. Every emissions schedule, fee split, and governance vote quietly writes a theory of the human person into code. Pretending software is morally neutral simply means inheriting yesterday’s theory without ever questioning whether it still serves life.

Imperfect experiments pointing somewhere kinder

Sacred economics will never arrive as one spotless mega-protocol that heals the world overnight. What actually moves me are smaller, messy, courageous experiments trying to test a different relational intuition in live water.

Take Circles. Instead of treating monetary creation as a monopoly handed down from central banks or venture cap tables, it lets ordinary people issue personal currency backed by mutual webs of human trust—turning money from an anonymous counter into a visible map of who actually vouches for whom.

"A money commons is a counter-movement against financialization."
— Circles UBI Handbook

Circles still wrestles with liquidity, onboarding, and scale, yet its core intuition lingers: reciprocity and local memory deserve a seat inside economic life. I feel a similar pulse in Gitcoin Grants and Optimism’s retroactive funding experiments. Both wrestle openly with gaming, narrative capture, and voter burnout—yet simply declaring that maintenance, documentation, and shared benefit deserve direct economic return already shifts the civilizational posture away from pure speculation.

What real gift economies actually ask of us

When engineers hear the word "gift," they often picture vague goodwill with zero accountability. Anthropologists know the opposite is true. Real gift economies are rich with memory, mutual obligation, and social awareness—they notice closely who shows up to repair the roof and who only arrives for the feast. Open ledgers can lend a hand here by preserving shared memory and making long-term contribution visible across seasons.

At the same time, I have to watch my own builder’s instinct to turn every warm human gesture into a quantifiable score. Some of the most vital threads holding a community together—quiet mediation during a painful conflict, translating dense research for newcomers, checking in on a burned-out teammate—resist clean metrics precisely because their meaning lives in care. Good infrastructure supports that work without trying to reduce it to a leaderboard.

Questioning the religion of number-go-up

Eisenstein’s return to demurrage—currency that gently loses value when hoarded so it stays in motion—feels almost scandalous inside a culture that worships early entry and diamond hands. Even if few large protocols adopt explicit negative interest soon, the intuition underneath can breathe through practical mechanisms today: protocol fees that continuously replenish public-goods pools, treasury charters favoring active stewardship over passive idling, local currencies built for circulation rather than speculation, and retroactive rewards for the patient maintenance markets chronically underprice.

Holding the cypherpunk tension with open eyes

Here is where metacognitive honesty matters most to me, because romantic language about "community" can drift into subtle coercion if we aren’t careful. One of cryptography’s truest moral gifts is the protection of privacy and voluntary exit—giving vulnerable people under predatory or fragile regimes a way to protect their dignity without asking permission from a neighbor or a state. I want nothing to do with compulsory intimacy or surveillance dressed up as solidarity.

The real craft lives right in the tension between those two loves: cypherpunk tools that guard autonomy and exit, paired with economic designs that make generosity, reciprocity, and long-term care feel natural and honored rather than naive.

"The new story of money is a story of reunion, reunion with the community of all life on earth."
— Charles Eisenstein

Cryptographic systems will never earn their place in history merely by settling trades a few milliseconds faster than legacy banks. They earn it when they remind us that an economy was never supposed to be a cold engine for endless accumulation—it is a living agreement about how we hold one another through time.
